I did my first wood firing in December, then followed up with another one in March and organized a final one in that smallest kiln in April. Somehow I love all of it, the sawdust, all kinds of kiln dust, the heat, the hours spent. 

Perhaps it's how easily the hours flow by as I stare at the clock and the temperature gauge, occassionally looking at how the fire is burning. 

It's how I am drawn back again to understand a bit more of how to operate this arcane and complex technology that is fundamental to human civilization, just so I can create pieces that are fun to look at. 

Perhaps I love it because of the conceptual distance between wood fired ceramics and predictable and mass produced items.  

![the darkside of the moon](/assets/img/darkside.jpg)

I started designing pots that would celebrate and amplify the nature of wood firing. Wood firing is directional, the fire is on one side so the flame and ash only 'blows' from that side. Depending on placement in the kiln some pots get more ash that others. 

The moon pot naturally has a 'dark side' that was facing away from the fire. The craters and landscapes are fanciful and exaagerated to hightlight the effects of the kiln atmosphere on the clay. 

![inside of sardine can](/assets/img/can1.jpg)

The sardine can is a heavy, handbuilt piece to withstand the rigors of wood firing. It's unlikely to blow over or warp during the process. A test ring next to it exploded during the firing, helpfully adding to the detrius I had deliberately placed inside. 

![drippy pot](/assets/img/drips.jpg) 

Using surface details to 'catch' glaze drips by creating overhangs is not unique to wood firing, just in this case it was quite effective and has a mezmerizing effect. 

The summer has come around and it is now much more arduous to spend hours and days next to a very hot kiln. I am sure I will spend the next few months dreaming of the fire and designing for the next firing season.
